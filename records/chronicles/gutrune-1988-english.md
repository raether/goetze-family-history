# Gutrune Falckon’s Goetze–Roemmler family chronicle

*English reading edition of the 1988 manuscript, followed by an editorial reader’s guide.*

[German original and English translation (PDF)](gutrune-1988-german-and-english.pdf) · [Source note and manuscript page guide](gutrune-1988.md) · [Biography index](../../people/README.md) · [Reader’s guide](#readers-guide)

Here I am writing down what I heard and saw of our family history as a child and in later years, and what I read in the family tree.

Gutrune Falckon, née Goetze. October 1988.

[Handwritten note in English: “Written by Gutrune Falckon, Albrecht Goetze’s sister. Received by Dieter Goetze, Albrecht’s son, on March 8, 1989.”]

My father, Rudolf Emil Goetze, was one of the sons of Emil Hermann Goetze, a factory owner in Glauchau, Saxony, and his wife Friederike Brunner, who was born in Frankfurt am Main.

## The earliest Goetzes and Brunners known to us

**Related biographies** · [Emil Hermann Götze](../../people/goetze/emil-hermann-goetze.md) · [Albertine Friedericke Caroline Brunner](../../people/brunner/friedericke-brunner.md) · [Bernhard Götze](../../people/goetze/bernhard-goetze.md)

Moritz Goetze, farmer in Kuhschnappel (“cow’s mouth”) near Eger. As far as I know, Eger now belongs to Czechoslovakia. 1550.

Adam Brunner of Eger married Dorothea Schweitzer in Frankfurt am Main on November 3, 1589.

Martin Goetze, farmer in Kuhschnappel, born 1570, died 1651.

Johann Brunner, shoemaker, born in Frankfurt am Main in 1631.

Georg Goetze, farmer in Kuhschnappel near Eger, 1619–1667.

Hans Adam Goetze, farmer and gardener in Kuhschnappel, 1669–1720.

Peter Goetze, linen weaver in Truenzig, born in Kuhschnappel in 1697, died in Truenzig in 1770.

Georg Friedrich Goetze, master weaver in Truenzig, 1728–1778.

Johann Michael Goetze, born in Truenzig in 1750. Master weaver in Crimitschau, later in Glauchau. Died in 1829.

Carl Friedrich Goetze, master weaver and merchant in Glauchau, 1784–1859.

Emil Hermann Goetze, manufacturer in Glauchau, Saxony, 1827–1902. During the so-called “years of invention,” the use of power looms made him a millionaire.

Emil Hermann Goetze married Friederike Brunner in Frankfurt am Main on November 18, 1857. Friederike was the daughter of Lorenz Gottlieb Brunner (1805–1885), a pharmacist in Frankfurt am Main, and his wife Anna Maria Enders, who was born in 1807 and died in 1844.

The Enders were prosperous poultry dealers in Frankfurt. My brother Albrecht had some very pretty small portraits of the Enders family. There was no photography in those days. People who could afford it had their portraits painted. Those portraits are probably in Alle’s family archive in Las Vegas, New Mexico, U.S.A., 621 Tecolote Street, ZIP 87701.

I remember visiting two kindly old ladies named Enders in Frankfurt with my mother when I was a child. We had been living in Heppenheim an der Bergstrasse since 1908, and Heppenheim was only an hour from Frankfurt by express train.

It is remarkable that the Goetze and Brunner families had known each other since the mid-1500s, despite the great distances in those days. There were no railways, only horses and horse-drawn mail coaches, or a walking stick. Frankfurt is in the midwest near the Rhine, and Eger lay on Germany’s eastern border.

Both families worked their way slowly toward prosperity over the generations. Having become wealthy, Emil Goetze went to Frankfurt to marry Friederike Brunner, whose father was a chemist and pharmacist. Her mother had died at only thirty-seven.

I remember my grandmother Friederike as a slightly stooped older lady dressed in black, with a little black bonnet on her head. She came to visit once when my mother and we children were living in Darmstadt. Father was an assistant at the state hospital and care institution for nervous disorders in Heppenheim an der Bergstrasse, preparing for the Hessian state examination so that he could become a senior physician there.

She gave us children chocolate and was very kind. She asked where I had gotten my big black cherry eyes. I was only four years old. It must have been 1907. When she arrived, she first washed her hands thoroughly and then, just to be safe and with a little smile, asked Mother whether Father’s mood was bearable. Mother reassured her that Father only came on Sundays.

She visited us again later in Heppenheim, when Father was a senior physician there. But she and her son Rudolf could never get along. Father was a freethinker, and Grandmother was very devout and very forceful. For instance, she burned the second part of Goethe’s Faust in the kitchen stove because she considered it immoral.

She left her big old Bible to Gertraute, who in turn left it to Dieter, Albrecht’s son, in Santa Barbara, California. She also left a short account of her youth in Frankfurt, which is probably with Alle. In it she describes in particular the great student uprising of 1848 in Frankfurt am Main. Frankfurt was a free city at the time. The German princes and the parliament assembled there to elect the first German emperor. Germany had separated from Austria, whose emperor had until then been the ruler over all the German princes. German intellectuals had already begun agitating for freer laws much earlier, about 1837, when the king of Hanover [dismissed] seven of his professors over a dispute about the Hanoverian constitution. The Brothers Grimm were two of them.

The uprisings reached their height in Frankfurt in 1849, with barricades and deaths. The student Blum was shot by the city as an insurgent. Many of the so-called “Forty-Niners” emigrated to America. The German princes then chose the king of Prussia as German emperor.

[The preceding account reflects Gutrune’s recollection as written.]

Friederike Goetze, née Brunner, outlived her husband by about ten years. She was born in 1830. I do not know exactly when she died. It seems to have been in 1912. Her maid Marie found her dead in bed one morning. There had been no sign of serious illness. It may have been heart failure. Her fortune had been almost entirely spent.

Friederike Goetze, née Brunner, left four children, one daughter and three sons. A fourth son had died in childhood.

Frieda Holz. The only daughter, Frieda, married into the Holz family. She and her children lived for a long time in the same apartment building in Gera as her mother. Frieda was widowed. She had three sons, Werner, Helmuth, and one whose name I cannot remember. [Handwritten: “Wolfgang?” A marginal note says Helmuth later became a lawyer in Munich.]

Bruno. The eldest son lived on private means. He had a villa on Lake Starnberg near Munich, or so I heard as a child. No one in the family seems to know anything more about him or his life. The general view is that he did not get along with the others. Even the villa on Lake Starnberg seems to be unknown to the family. See the closing remarks under “New York Branch,” page 35.

Rudolf, my father. He was very musical and wanted to be a professional pianist. Because his ear for pitch was not one hundred percent reliable, he became a physician specializing in nervous disorders. He founded the “Richard Wagner and Nietzsche Museum” in Eisenach, Saxony. It probably no longer exists.

Bernhard, the youngest, was a merchant. He inherited his father’s factory. Because his siblings wanted their shares of the inheritance paid out, he had to sell the factory and became a wholesaler for textile mills. He then lived in Berlin, married to Johanna Roemmler, the youngest sister of my mother, Elsa. See page 18, Bernhard and Hannchen’s family.

## My father, Dr. Rudolf Emil Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Rudolf Emil Fritz Götze](../../people/goetze/rudolf-goetze.md)

Born in Glauchau, Saxony, September 23, 1863. Died in Alzey, Rhenish Hesse, October 30, 1920. Married Else Roemmler, the third daughter of Commercial Councillor Roemmler of Dresden, in Dresden on April 9, 1892.

Because he was the cleverest of the three sons, Rudolf was sent to Dresden at the age of ten to attend the best Gymnasium in Germany. He lived in a boarding house for boys. Perhaps that is one reason he had so little contact with his family. His mother was also somewhat puritanical and did not pamper her children. “Little bites of cheese for the dwarfs” at supper became a family saying. (Three big boys!)

After his father’s death he used his inheritance to buy land in Naunhof near Leipzig, where he built his sanatorium for nervous disorders. It consisted of two houses in the very modern Art Nouveau style. The little town of Naunhof apparently lacked an adequate water supply, so he and a friend [named Seifert in the margin] financed the building of a water tower. The project became much more costly than my father had expected. He also signed on his partner’s behalf. The venture failed for lack of money. One day the partner disappeared, leaving his own wife and children and my father in the lurch. My father had put up his real estate as security for him. The partner escaped into the “Foreign Legion, Africa.” My father lost his entire property and everything else.

Fortunately, my parents were close friends with Leipzig’s wealthy and intellectual families. Leipzig was then a center of culture for wealthy merchants and artists. The Gewandhaus was the most famous concert hall in Germany at the time. The writer Wedekind was one of Leipzig’s cultural luminaries. So was a sculptor whose name I no longer remember. (He made a well-known bust of Salomé, whom Nietzsche was courting at the time.) My father, himself a wealthy young man and a devotee of Wagner, was well known in these circles, including for his work as a physician.

My parents also knew the Otto family, wholesale merchants who imported furs from Russia. Curiously, it turned out much, much later that the mother of the man I would marry was named Marie Otto. She was a daughter of the Leipzig wholesale merchant Otto. I have a picture of her as a child.

In the course of the First and Second World Wars, the city’s inner and outward riches gradually scattered to the winds.

Long before the collapse, Father went to Italy for three months with his friend and patient, the lawyer Hezel. (Hezel had once defended Liebknecht.) My mother was left on her own for three months with six children, a sanatorium, and many employees, including a gardener and a kindergarten teacher.

In that wealthy and artistic milieu, the machine factory owner Max Krause and his wife Laura became my parents’ closest friends and remained so until death. Max Krause paid the balance of the debts, which were still quite large after everything had been sold. My eldest brother Hermann later told me that the people who bought the water tower project cheaply at the time grew rich from it.

Uncle Krause also paid for part of the sons’ education and other expenses later on, whenever necessary. My brothers repaid most of the debt during the inflation. There was still a balance that we women, Mother, Gertraute, and I, wanted to pay off. But Uncle Krause visited us in Spandau shortly before the Second World War (Aunt Laura had long since died) and forgave the rest of the debt. Fortunately, Uncle Krause died before the Second World War, so he did not live to see Germany’s collapse. My mother had nursed Aunt Laura through her final illness until she died.

Then Mother came to us, meaning Gertraute and me, in Berlin to raise my sister’s little son. [The final word is cut off in the text.]

After Father lost his fortune we moved to Hesse. Father became an assistant at the Hessian State Asylum in Heppenheim an der Bergstrasse. Mother moved to Darmstadt with the six children and rented a ground-floor apartment. When the moving van arrived, Mother had no money to pay. She telegraphed her father, but he refused to pay. I was only four and cannot remember it. Since the mover refused to unload the furniture, we must have slept on the apartment floor. In any event, we did move in, and I assume Uncle Krause sent the money.

After Father passed the Hessian state examination for physicians specializing in nervous disorders, he became senior physician of the men’s division at the institution. The place was idyllic. There were wonderful parks and gardens in lovely countryside at the edge of the Odenwald. The director at the time belonged to a bird protection society. In spring the nightingales sang day and night. Everything bloomed as if it were paradise. The director, Bieberbach with the bushy beard, and his wife were very cultured people. They liked children. Aunt Biebi gave me sweets and read me fairy tales. They loved to hear Father play on our Bechstein grand. It was idyllic.

Then, unfortunately, came the First World War. After the war a new director arrived, and of course my rather hot-tempered father could not get along with him. He was transferred to Alzey in Rhenish Hesse as a disciplinary measure. Shortly after the move he developed a stomach ulcer that he would not let them operate on. He died in 1920 shortly after his transfer. He was fifty-seven and had spent twelve years in the Hessian state service.

He was a man of great contradictions, a fabulous pianist and an idealist. Very intelligent, he foresaw Germany’s downfall as early as 1914. Yet living with him was very difficult because he was prone to finding fault and had a fierce temper. I think that deep down he was not at peace with himself because of the many small and large disappointments that life brought him. After all, he had once been a wealthy young man with great hopes.

## The Roemmler family

**Related biographies** · [Carl Emil Römmler](../../people/roemmler/emil-roemmler.md) · [Else Römmler](../../people/roemmler/else-roemmler.md) · [Johanna Römmler](../../people/roemmler/johanna-roemmler.md)

The earliest Roemmler ancestors known to us are Jakob Romelin, who died in 1621, and Johann Peter Romelin, born in 1683 in Wimpfen am Neckar. [Handwritten family-tree references appear to read X 513 beside Jakob, IX 257 Leonhard, VIII 129 Johann Georg, and VII 65 beside Johann Peter. A note says Johann Peter died in Mitweida, Saxony.]

Wimpfen belonged to Hesse, in the district of Heppenheim an der Bergstrasse. We lived in Heppenheim for twelve years. To my father’s amusement, Johann had emigrated to Saxony and become a town musician in Mitweida! My parents even traveled to Wimpfen once and took me along. It is a charming country town in a splendid landscape.

A Johann Georg Rumelin was a farrier in Wimpfen am Neckar and died there on November 22, 1689.

My mother Elsa, née Roemmler, was one of the five daughters of Commercial Councillor Karl Emil Roemmler, a photographer and factory owner in Dresden. He was born in Mitweida in 1842 and died in Dresden in 1941, at nearly ninety-nine. His father, Karl Gottlob Roemmler, born in Mitweida in 1815, was a master weaver, portrait painter, and photographer.

His mother was Wilhelmine Auguste Irmscher, born in Mitweida in 1815 and married in 1838. She died of typhoid [German: Typhus] in middle age.

After his mother died, Emil left home and set out on his travels. His father sent him on his way with three thalers and a camera. Emil Roemmler wrote a short autobiography about his life and his remarkable success as a photographer. I read it in Raimund’s studio when I visited your grandparents in Mérida in 1973. You probably know these recollections of his youth.

He describes how, after arriving in Dresden, he walked through the park near the palace one sunny morning and met a group of children who were playing. He talked with them. They wanted to have their photograph taken and he obliged. The children turned out to be the king’s. He was then appointed court photographer and was later commissioned to photograph the priceless paintings of Dresden’s famous art galleries.

That was the beginning of his career as the owner of a fine-art printing business, first as Roemmler and Jonas and later as Roemmler without a partner. Emil Roemmler and Helene, née Arndt, married at twenty-one. They went straight to Paris, where Emil learned three-color photography for his printing business.

Helene Roemmler, née Arndt, was the daughter of Gottfried Ludwig Arndt, a portrait and house painter in Chemnitz, Saxony. The wedding took place in Chemnitz on February 16, 1862.

They became the parents of five beautiful daughters and three sons.

Kaete, born 1863, married Jean Pape, a professor at the Dresden School of Applied Arts.

Frieda, born 1867, married Alex Desberats, a merchant. Lene, born 1870, married Eugen Desberats, also a merchant. Alex and Eugen were brothers.

Else, born 1868, married Rudolf Goetze, a physician specializing in nervous disorders. Johanna, born 1872, married Bernhard Goetze, a merchant. Rudolf and Bernhard were brothers.

Hans Emil, born 1873, was a merchant and married Marie Huebner. Fritz, born 1877, was an officer and remained unmarried. Willy, born 1880, was an engineer and married Margarete Schubert.

Our grandmother Helene, unlike Grandfather, had a somewhat melancholy temperament and was a little clairvoyant. For example, on the day her son Fritz died she saw a saber flash. On that very day in 1914, her son Fritz, a Saxon cavalry captain, had been fatally struck by a bullet while on horseback in Alsace. [Handwritten: “World War I.”]

Helene died of lung cancer at eighty-two. My mother, already a widow, took care of her to the end.

I remember my grandparents’ beautiful villa well. As a child I went there with my mother for their golden wedding anniversary, which was celebrated with great pomp. I particularly remember the marble bathtub, built into the ground floor with marble steps. Grandmother is said to have fallen in once fully dressed. I also remember the charming summer house in rural Loschwitz, a health resort near Dresden. I went to their diamond wedding anniversary too, celebrated at a hotel in the snow-covered Ore Mountains. It was held a year early, since people knew Grandmother would soon die.

Grandfather Emil Roemmler remained very spry well into old age. He visited us in Spandau near Berlin around 1934. He had traveled from Dresden to Spandau on his own! There he was, umbrella over his shoulder, full of high spirits. Sadly, his final years were less happy. He had trusted his eldest son Hans with the business. Then came the inflation. The beautiful floral postcards he had produced himself no longer sold well enough, and neither did the gallery postcards. Eventually paper money was printed in enormous rolls. The big villa had long since been sold. He and Helene lived in a small apartment.

Helene fell gravely ill. My mother came and cared for her to the end. Mother then stayed with Grandfather a little longer. The older ladies of Dresden were all mad about him because they thought he was still rich.

The apartment was then given up, and he was given a room in his eldest son’s house. Full of life as ever, he began a passionate affair with the children’s governess. They met in the dark in the evenings and kissed so intensely that Grandfather had to go to the dentist the next day to have his last teeth pulled. That was how the story went around the family, to great laughter.

Then Uncle Hans declared bankruptcy and put his own finances back in order through it. That is, he remained a rich man. But the fortune that all of us would have inherited a share of had disappeared. Uncle Hans then sent letters begging the closest relatives to support Grandfather. He wrote, “A hundred marks is enough for the old man.” No one wanted to pay or could afford to. Finally his eldest daughter, the widow of a professor at the Dresden School of Applied Arts, took him in. He died peacefully there, thank God before the destruction [of Dresden]. [The last line is clipped in this scan and was checked against a second copy. A handwritten note gives his age as nearly ninety-nine.]

## The Arndt and Heinigke families

**Related biographies** · [Gottfried Ludwig Arndt](../../people/arndt/gottfried-ludwig-arndt.md) · [Helene Arndt](../../people/arndt/helene-arndt.md) · [Auguste Heinigke](../../people/heinigke/auguste-heinigke.md)

Helene Roemmler, née Arndt, was the daughter of Gottfried Ludwig Arndt, a portrait and house painter in Chemnitz, Saxony. He was born in Elbing, East Prussia, on June 18, 1810. It was then the last Prussian port town on the Baltic before the Polish border. He died in his son-in-law’s house in Dresden on February 4, 1902, at the age of ninety-two.

He married in Chemnitz at thirty-one, to seventeen-year-old Auguste Heinigke, who was born in Gablenz near Chemnitz on July 29, 1824, and died in Chemnitz on December 4, 1874, in her forty-ninth year. [The wedding year is typed as 1941. The ages suggest 1841. Her stated age at death also differs from the dates given.] They had two daughters, Helene, my maternal grandmother, and a younger daughter whose name I do not know.

Auguste was the grandmother of my mother, Else Roemmler. She was a very beautiful woman, as the enclosed pictures show. [Handwritten: “Very serious.”] My mother remembered that as a child visiting her she often saw Auguste stand on the balcony eating cherries and spitting the pits down onto the street.

Family lore has it that her husband was so jealous that he locked her in the house when he went off to work. His favorite daughter was Helene’s sister. On her deathbed Auguste said that the younger of her two daughters was the child of a Spaniard and that her husband was not the father. This caused such an uproar in the family that the younger daughter emigrated to America. There she became a faith healer (Christian Science). Unfortunately, I know nothing more about her. [See page 36.]

As I said, Gottfried Ludwig Arndt spent his old age in his son-in-law’s house in Dresden. My mother told me the following about him.

When my eldest brother Hermann was still little, Mother took him to Dresden to visit her parents. The boy had never seen a very old man and asked his mother in astonishment, “Who is that little old man?” From then on our great-grandfather was always called “the little old man.”

His five granddaughters, including my mother, did not like it when the little old man was always trying to kiss them. “Giving little kisses is my life, and there’s no sin in it,” he used to say. The young girls saw things differently. They liked to lock him in the wardrobe now and then. That was particularly easy at the summer house in Loschwitz. It had big cupboards built into the walls, with doors painted with colorful flowers. It was easy to lock someone in there.

After Grandmother Helene died, I visited my mother in Dresden, where she was keeping house for her father. There I saw the many family portraits and Gottfried Ludwig Arndt’s self-portrait. Some of these may be in my niece Alle Lee’s archive in Las Vegas, New Mexico, 621 Tecolote Street, ZIP 87701, U.S.A. Telephone 505-425-8174.

I remember Gottfried Arndt’s self-portrait very well. It showed a very handsome young man with black hair, a slightly curved aquiline nose, and fiery, very dark eyes. He looked to me more southern and Romance in appearance than German. Grandmother Helene, my mother, Auguste Arndt, and Albrecht all had that nose, probably inherited from the Arndts and Heinigkes.

The Heinigke family still lives in Altenburg, Saxony. My brother Albrecht visited them some time before he left Germany. They were always a highly respected family there: pastors, lawyers, and physicians. [See page 35, as noted in the margin.]

The earliest family members known to us are Georg Heinigke, citizen and tailor, 1599, and Christoph Heinigke, a pastor in Gera, born in Altenburg in 1606 and deceased in Gera in 1656.

The Heinigkes were related to the von Robas. [A handwritten correction suggests the spelling von Roda.]

The first “von Roba” entry in the family tree is Rolf Heinrich von Roba, pastor and adjunct at Boessneck around 1680. His son was apparently Johann Erasmus von Roba, born in Boessneck, pastor at Obernitz near Saalfeld, and married to Christina Loeber in Orlamuende in 1703.

The von Roba family, most of whom were Protestant pastors in Saxony, had probably been French Protestants (Huguenots or Waldensians) who somehow immigrated to Saxony with the help of the Heinigkes. [The suggestion is queried in the margin. A crossed-out line states that the von Roba family also knew the Pfeilers in Elbing.]

A Christiana Friederike von Roba, born in Altenburg in 1754 and deceased there in 1810, was married to Dr. Heinigke in Altenburg.

My mother told me that around 1740 the following happened. Johann Arendt arrived with a young girl in Elbing on the Baltic, the last Prussian port on the Polish border. They landed from a Swedish ship. No one knows where they came from. The young girl he brought with him was called De Noël or von Neaus. She was French. Johann Arendt learned the cloth-bleaching trade, settled in Elbing, became a citizen, and married Christina Kieseling. There are no dates. [Handwritten: “From the family tree one cannot tell whether the first letter of the name is an R or an N.”]

The girl who had immigrated with him was Maria Susanna von Neaus. She married Christoph Pfeiler, a merchant and cloth dealer, who was born in Elbing in 1725 and died there in 1778. She died in Elbing in 1806. [The marriage line has a crossed-out year and an underlined 1743.]

Gottfried Arndt, son of Johann Arendt, was a leather manufacturer in Elbing. He was born there in 1755 and died there in 1810. In Elbing in 1793 he married Maria Elisabeth Pfeiler, who was born in Elbing in 1768 and died there in 1823. She was probably a granddaughter of Maria Susanna von Neaus.

The name “von Neaus” appears without any date between Georg Samuel Pfeiler, an advocate in Elbing, married to Anna Marie Tiergart [spelling queried in the margin], 1716, and Dr. Friedrich Heinigke in Altenburg, 1684–1759. The entry reads “Johann von Neaus?” with question marks.

Ernst Adam Luther von Roba [a handwritten correction may read Roda], a pastor, was born in Obernitz near Saalfeld in 1718 and died in Nonstab in 1784.

Clearly these families had known one another for a long time [handwritten: “about 100 years!”]. They were all devout members of the Reformed faith and helped one another. It seems likely that Maria Susanna von Neaus was a Huguenot too [queried in the margin]. They were driven out of France because they joined the Reformation. France was strictly Catholic. Frederick the Great, king of Prussia (1740–1786), however, allowed them to settle in Prussia. Many of the French Protestants came to East Prussia as a result.

Johann Arendt, whose origins are unknown, brought Maria Susanna von Neaus to Elbing around 1740. [See page 36.] Gottfried Arndt’s son was Gottfried Ludwig Arndt, a house and portrait painter who married Auguste Heinigke in Chemnitz. She was the grandmother of my mother, Else Goetze-Roemmler.

## Hildegard, Erna, and Brigitte Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Hildegard Götze](../../people/goetze/hildegard-goetze.md) · [Erna Götze](../../people/goetze/erna-goetze.md) · [Brigitte Götze](../../people/goetze/brigitte-goetze.md)

Hildegard Goetze was born in Glauchau in 1894. She married Frank Glatzel in Berlin on November 27, 1918. They divorced. They had two daughters, Ingeborg and Barbara. Hilde is now ninety-six and lives in Berlin. Both daughters are married.

Erna Goetze was born in Glauchau in 1896. She married Werner Holz, a merchant born in Merano in 1891. They divorced in 1936. Werner was the eldest son of Frieda Holz, née Goetze, the only sister of my father, Rudolf Goetze. We are related to Erna and her children in three ways. Erna and Werner had two children, a daughter, Renate, and a son, Juergen. Their daughter Renate Heidelberg lives in Munich and is very knowledgeable about family research. Her address is Dornbergstr. 10, 8000 Munich 80. Erna is now ninety-three and lives in Berlin near Ernst Wilhelm and Irmgard Schmitt. Irmgard is Hermann’s daughter. They have become friends.

Brigitte Goetze was born in Glauchau in 1901. In 1926 she married Hubert Spieler, a veterinarian. He died in 1976 in Weissberg, East Germany. She died by suicide in 1977. [The typewritten line gives Frankfurt am Main. A handwritten note says “a year later in Weissenberg.”] They had two sons, Reinhard, a television cameraman, and Herbert, a photographer in Zittau.

Hubert Spieler’s brother had a farm in South West Africa. Reinhard emigrated and took over the farm. He later sold it to his neighbor there and returned to East Germany. [A marginal note mentions the family of General von Seidlitz, spelling uncertain.] Later he settled in West Germany.

When they were children my older siblings were very fond of the three girls. There is even a little poem by Hermann: “Dear Uncle, please, please send us Hilde, Erna, and Brigitte.” They visited us often. As young girls Hilde and Erna were each sent to stay with us for a year to learn to cook from my mother. That was in Heppenheim. In fun they called my mother “Aunt Living-Off-the-Fat” (Tante Fettleben).

I also remember visiting them in Berlin with Mother as a child. Brigitte was only two years older than I was. Uncle Bernhard gave us little arithmetic problems and found that neither of us could do arithmetic at all! Brigitte said grace at the main meal. To her father’s great amusement, she had long since made the prayer fit her own understanding: “All the good forks and all we tuck in, dear God, all come from You. We thank You for it.”

Because Brigitte was much younger than Hilde and Erna, she lived through the worst of the hunger in Berlin in the First World War with her parents. She loved horses. After the war her father gave her a beautiful riding horse. They were living in Gruenheide near Berlin then. That was also where she married Hubert Spieler, a veterinarian. Sadly, they lived through the Second World War in Saxony and died in old age in East Germany. The many hard times they had lived through had evidently become too much for Brigitte.

## Dr. Hermann Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Hermann Götze](../../people/goetze/hermann-goetze.md)

Born in Würzburg on April 3, 1893. Died in Berlin on May 12, 1962.

He was very gifted, good-hearted, and a little nervous. Languages, drawing, and painting were his chief interests. He was to train for a position in the Foreign Office. As soon as he finished secondary school he was sent to Rome to learn Italian, later to Paris, then to the International Institute in Berlin to learn Turkish, Arabic, and so on, and to university to study law. He did his one-year voluntary military service in 1914 and went straight into the war. He spent five years in military service and could only finish his training afterward. He served first in the artillery.

Before the war, as a pupil at the Gymnasium in Bensheim an der Bergstrasse, he made friends with a number of classmates and brought them all to our home in Heppenheim. My parents were very hospitable in those days and were also friends with the town’s leading people, including the notary Metz and his family and district councillor von Hahn and his wife.

Among the school friends was Egon, one of the seven sons of Pastor Steiner in Seeheim an der Bergstrasse. Even as a senior schoolboy, Egon was a fabulous pianist and violinist. We had lovely parties with performances in the music room. Father and Egon played four hands on our Bechstein grand, or Father played the grand and Egon accompanied him on the violin.

Sadly, Egon later had a very tragic and short life. His father, a real tyrant at home, would not allow him to study music. Much to his disgust, he was made to become a doctor. After the war he became a doctor in Berlin, after failing his examination twice. He married a very charming, unusual woman, an artistic type, who sadly died of tuberculosis. After her death Egon poisoned himself. That was the end of this gifted young man.

Another of Hermann’s classmates was Erich van Hoboken-Schmidt. A wealthy Dutchman and his wife had bought a magnificent property in Alsbach an der Bergstrasse and settled there. They adopted the four orphaned children of a senior teacher named Schmidt in Wiesbaden. He had died of diabetes. His wife was also dead, from a cause unknown to me. Of course my parents invited all four children, who were already young people by then. They took a great interest in them, especially as Mrs. van Hoboken had also died. Little Gertrud, the youngest, had grown up from the age of eleven with only her three brothers and the old uncle.

Uncle Hoboken was an easygoing, Dutch-speaking, one-legged older man. He usually sat in an armchair at a large table, a talking parrot on his shoulder and a Saint Bernard and a fox terrier at his feet. Gertrud’s three brothers were Karl, Erich, and Rudi.

When Hermann and Erich were in their final year of school they were sent to the customary dancing lessons in Bensheim. The younger siblings went along as well: Rudi and Gertrud, and from the Goetzes, Hermann and Gertraute. My mother was chaperone to the two young girls. Karl did not go to the dancing lessons. He was already studying astronomy at Heidelberg University. The lessons led to the later engagements of Hermann and Gertrud, and Rudi and Gertraute.

The First World War put an end to all those plans. Hermann, who served in the artillery, was sent straight to the Eastern Front with all the school friends in the same regiment. He spent Christmas 1914 in the ice and snow in Poland, lost in a snowstorm with his gun and his comrades. Later he wrote home. He had ended up in a military hospital. He had come down with dysentery. Later, as a lieutenant, he was transferred to Turkey. He served as a Turkish interpreter in the Dardanelles. While on leave he married Gertrud van Hoboken-Schmidt. On September 29, 1918, Irmgard was born in Alsbach.

Hermann came home from the war in the winter of 1918. The dream of the Foreign Office had evaporated. There was no Foreign Office anymore. Uncle van Hoboken had died. The Alsbach property was sold. The proceeds lost their value, and there was just enough left to start over slowly and with great difficulty.

Hermann, Gertrud, and Irmgard moved in with us in Father’s official residence. That was then prohibited, and they had to rent an apartment in Heppenheim. Hermann went to Heidelberg University every day to complete his doctorate in law. My father died. Hermann and Gertrud moved to Berlin. My mother stayed with little Irmgard in the apartment on Graeffstrasse in Heppenheim.

Uncle Bernhard, my father’s brother, trained Hermann in Berlin as a sales representative for textiles. They had almost no money left. Hermann was so starved that he nearly fainted on a streetcar. Then he got a position as an auditor and slowly worked his way up to prosperity. He founded his own business and had several employees. He worked chiefly for the largest book publishers.

They were doing well. Then came the Second World War. Thank God, they did not lose their home to bombing. At that time they recovered quickly. They moved to a very beautiful apartment near the Grunewald. The business did well. He died of a stroke in a Berlin hospital in 1962 at the age of sixty-nine. His hobby was astrology. When I visited them before leaving for America, he showed me marvelous, very expensive large books on astrology with many pictures. He said to me, “Just wait, we shall have a new age of inwardness again.” Gertrud listened with a disdainful smile.

## Gertraute, 1895–1981

**Related biographies** · [Gertraute Götze](../../people/goetze/gertraute-goetze.md)

Gertraute was a very lively, beautiful child who liked to climb the tallest trees. What she loved most was playing with cats, putting doll clothes on them and taking them out for rides. She wrote very pretty poems from an early age and was Father’s favorite. When she was only eight, he took her to a box at the Leipzig Opera to hear and see Richard Wagner’s Die Meistersinger.

Unfortunately, at about twelve she developed a serious hip condition that was thought at the time to be tubercular. She was in bed for a long time and then wore a long brace on her left leg for four years. By sixteen she had healed so well that she could walk normally and even dance, despite a slight limitation in her left hip. She was sent to dancing lessons with the other young people. That was the beginning of her long romance with Rudi van Hoboken, who at the time was still a schoolboy. Later Rudi did not have to serve in the war. He had been deaf in one ear since having scarlet fever in childhood. He wanted to be a painter, and Uncle van Hoboken sent him to the Academy of Fine Arts in Munich. He soon broke off his engagement to Gertraute. She was then sent to Aunt Krause in Leipzig. She attended the Lette School for women in Leipzig and learned foreign-language business correspondence. She later worked in Heidelberg, Frankfurt, Munich, and finally Berlin.

In 1926 I came to Berlin to live with her. Her son Franzl was born in 1927. She spent the last four months before his birth in the home of our brother Gottfried in Landsberg an der Warthe. Meanwhile I had taken a nine-month course in therapeutic exercise [handwritten: “That was then the first course of its kind in Germany”] and was looking for an apartment for us in Berlin. Our mother came and we found a pretty ground-floor apartment in a newly built villa in Marienfelde. My sister came from Landsberg with the baby. She soon went back to work as a commercial clerk, later in the library of the Altes Museum.

Franzl was the second grandchild my mother raised. Irmgard was the first. Later we moved to Spandau near Berlin, into a newly built apartment with central heating. Franzl was a darling, very delicate and highly intelligent child. My mother and all of us loved him dearly. Mother often said, “This child is a genius.” But he died suddenly of diphtheria in 1934 at the age of seven. Grandmother was so shaken that she became seriously ill and died in 1935 of an intestinal tumor she had apparently had for many years. [Handwritten in English: “67 years old.”]

Long before, while visiting our old friends and benefactors Laura and Max Krause in Leipzig, she had walked in the dark through a room that had a large hole in the floor. She fell into the room below and landed on her tailbone. It healed and caused her no trouble for at least twelve years.

By 1935 great changes were already underway. Albrecht, who had been a member of the Social Democratic Party, had had to flee to America. He was now a professor at Yale University in the United States and had sent for his family. His son Dieter had been with us in Spandau for about half a year. Albrecht urged Gertraute and me to come to him. I still wanted to stay in Berlin to see how everything would develop. So Gertraute left, and I stayed alone in a room in Berlin with friends of my sister’s.

Gertraute stayed with Albrecht and Friedl for the first year. Friedl had been Gertraute’s friend before marrying Albrecht. Then Gertraute went to New York. She became secretary to a German-Jewish firm she had already known in Germany. She lived in a German boardinghouse with many refugees from Europe, including former members of the youth movement. There she met Walter Fischer. They married.

As the threat of war in Europe drew closer, I immigrated to America in 1938 and lived for four years in this refuge too. Then I married Theo Falcke, an acquaintance of Gertraute and Walter. The Fischers followed us to California later, settling in San Francisco while we were in Los Angeles. Walter worked as an electrician’s helper in a shipyard and my sister in an office. Toward the end of the war they bought ten acres of scrubland very cheaply in Oroville, near the beautiful Feather River Canyon. They built a little house and grew their own vegetables, oranges, and so on. They lived there in some seclusion for many years, until Walter suddenly sold everything against his wife’s wishes. Unfortunately he sold very cheaply, just before land prices rose sharply.

Gertraute grew increasingly ill. She could only walk on crutches. In the end I took her into my house. Theo had died in 1971. I nursed Gertraute in my home for three and a half years. Walter came too, cooked, and tended the garden. It was a very hard time. Age, illness, and life’s disappointments had apparently unsettled both of them and made them a little odd. Gertraute died of pneumonia on November 18, 1981, at eighty-six and a half.

I then found Walter a place he could pay for with his Social Security. It is a very nice retirement home, only ten minutes from my house. He still lives there and will be ninety in December 1988. (All his life he would rather have died!)

## Albrecht Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Albrecht Götze](../../people/goetze/albrecht-goetze.md)

Born in Leipzig on January 11, 1897. Died in Garmisch-Partenkirchen on August 15, 1971.

He was the most professionally successful of the six children. As a boy he skipped a grade and finished secondary school early so that he could start studying ancient languages. Unfortunately, he was soon called up for military service. He was assigned to the infantry and went to the Western Front. He became a lieutenant. At the Battle of the Somme in France, he was shot in the leg. Retreating soldiers dragged him back with them, and he spent a long time in a military hospital. The leg healed but remained shorter than the other. He had to wear an orthopedic shoe for the rest of his life, but he could walk freely again, dance, and ride.

After the war he continued his studies and became a professor of Assyriology in Heidelberg and later in Marburg, Upper Hesse. Hitler came to power. Because Albrecht was a Social Democrat and also protested the persecution of Jews (he wrote an article in the Frankfurter Zeitung), he was persecuted by the Nazi Party and had to leave Germany. His wife and children were only allowed to follow him after he obtained a non-quota visa for the United States.

He was appointed to Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut, as an Assyriologist. That was where his success began. Every summer he worked in Central Asia, deciphering the hieroglyphs on the tombs of peoples long gone (Hittites and others). The Arab sheikhs liked him very much. They gave him the title “Honorary Sheikh.” He was often their guest.

He once told me about the fantastic banquets the Arab sheikhs invited him to. A picture of Albrecht in white sheikh’s robes appeared in a New York newspaper. He spoke Arabic and rode into the deserts with Arabs to the various excavations. He told me that once, just as he mounted his horse to set out with a group of Arabs, the horse bolted. Everyone was horrified and thought they would never see the professor again. But lo and behold, back he came, quite at ease on the horse’s back and laughing too. That won them over. He soon became known and liked among the Arabs as a good rider and a good companion. In Baghdad, though, he was once taken for a Jew and had to run for his life.

He became president of the large international society for the study of antiquity. He became famous in his field. He even went to Australia once for an international conference in his field. He also traveled to Venezuela to visit Raimund. He was held in high esteem at Yale. See the obituaries when he died. [Crossed out: “Unfortunately, his marriage was not very happy.”] When I visited Raimund and Louise in Venezuela in January 1973, we talked about Albrecht, and Raimund said, “He was a fine fellow.”

Albrecht died of a stroke in the Bavarian Alps. He was nearly seventy-five. His youthful love, whom he never forgot, was his cousin Irene from Stuttgart. Sadly, Irene’s life ended early and dramatically. She had given him the blue sapphire ring he wore while she was on her deathbed.

## Dr. Gottfried Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Gottfried Götze](../../people/goetze/gottfried-goetze.md)

Biologist. Degree in agriculture. Director of the beekeeping school at Mayen in the Rhineland and later professor at the University of Bonn. Born in Naunhof near Leipzig on April 4, 1898. Died in 1965. Married Margret Ulbrich at Berlin-Lichtenberg on March 23, 1923. Margret Ulbrich was born June 6, 1895, and died in Monschau in 1980 [last digit faint].

When I last visited Germany, in 1972, I also visited Gottfried’s widow Margret in Godesberg near Bonn. She said to me then, “You were Gottfried’s first love.” He was always very good to me. He was five years older than I was, but as a child I always went with him on his Sunday walks through the surrounding woods. Our favorite was the walk to the Bombach valley, an idyllic little wooded valley that played a mystical role in the dreams of his later years. At the Institute for Animal Psychology near Bonn, of which he later became director, an almost life-size picture of me as a child hung above his desk. I am sitting on a bench under the big beeches, looking out over the Bombach valley. On our long walks he often told me fairy tales of his own invention about dwarfs, elves, and woodland animals.

After taking the so-called emergency school-leaving examination, he was immediately called up for military service at seventeen. We were in the middle of the First World War. He soon went to the Western Front to lay electrical cables. He spent his nights in a trench. I wrote him many letters then, and he always answered them. He returned from the war unwounded and studied agriculture and the natural sciences. Raimund helped support him financially. Raimund had not been called up, having been deferred because of physical frailty. He had, however, received orders to report for military service curiously, for the very day the armistice was declared. Of course he went to the registration office anyway, but was sent straight home. He was already a salaried assistant at the Einstein Institute in Frankfurt am Main when the three older brothers had to resume their studies.

Gottfried studied agriculture first. He took part in one of the settlement communities then in vogue, Bergfried in Upper Bavaria. My mother, by then a widow, also took part, as did many other idealistic Germans. (People contributed either money or labor.) The head of the settlement was a White Russian who turned out to be a swindler and disappeared one day with the funds.

Gottfried then took his examinations for a degree in agriculture, became assistant to the manager of a large farm in Upper Hesse, and married Margret Ulbrich. He had met her as a senior pupil at the independent school community in Oberhambach, where she taught the little ones. Hans Christoph was born. Gottfried continued his studies at the university in Giessen, Upper Hesse, and took his doctorate in natural science.

He then became assistant professor at the university in Landsberg an der Warthe, later director of the beekeeping school in Maien in the Rhön [as written here, see “Mayen in the Rhineland” on page 27], and after the Second World War professor of natural science in Bonn. They moved to Godesberg near Bonn, into a pretty little apartment overlooking the garden of the English legation [marginal note, reading partly uncertain].

Margret and he had two sons and five daughters. The daughters were Bettina, Hella, Lore, Gudrun, and Renate. The sons were Hans-Christoph and Martin, the youngest. Gottfried was almost blind and died after several strokes at the age of sixty-seven. [Handwritten changes to the typed account of an eye condition and strokes.]

## Dr. Raimund Goetze

**Related biographies** · [Raimund Götze](../../people/goetze/raimund-goetze.md)

Physicist. Assistant at the Einstein Institute, Frankfurt am Main. Teacher in Barcelona. Professor at the Universidad de los Andes, Mérida, Venezuela. Born October 8, 1899, in Naunhof near Leipzig. Married Louise Lagrange in Torelló, Barcelona, on September 16, 1943 [date as typed, which differs from the sequence on page 30].

Raimund was closest to me in age. During the war we soon became the only children still at home. Of course we got into every kind of mischief. We climbed into an apartment and got out through the window, sliding down the lightning rod. It burned the palms of our hands terribly. We climbed up the high, wide stone wall around the institution and ran all the way around on top of it. We looked down into the courtyard of the women’s section, where the agitated women ran about screaming.

We went up to the Starkenburg and climbed around the ruins. To our great fright we came across a big eagle owl, which looked us over sleepily and suspiciously. We built ourselves a nest in a big walnut tree in the institution’s nursery and did our homework there. [In the margin: “But Raimund did all my algebra and geometry homework!”] I took my tame grass snake up with me. It climbed around the branches and basked in the sun. We had found it in the Bombach valley one spring when it had just come out of hibernation.

Then Raimund finished secondary school and went away too, to study at the university in Tübingen and later in Göttingen. After his doctorate in physics he became an assistant at the Einstein Institute in Frankfurt. The war was over. Germany had become a Social Democratic republic.

Then came the setback. From his last year of secondary school onward, Raimund had done nothing but study. That explained his rapid success and his early doctorate. On top of this came malnutrition and the constant agitation of the war years. He developed double vision. He gave up his position and hiked through Italy with a group of young people. Later he went to Casablanca, where he worked as a photographer, and then to Spain. He became a teacher in Barcelona and married Louise Lagrange, a Frenchwoman from Rombas in Alsace-Lorraine. Louise also spoke German and even wrote us letters in German. Raimund kept up a steady correspondence with our mother all those years.

Raimund and Louise became the parents of twin daughters, Yvonne and Rolanda. Sadly our mother had died shortly before and did not live to share this great event with us. Then came the Spanish Civil War. Raimund managed with great difficulty to get himself and his family to safety in France. I visited him there. It was truly rural, a farmhouse with clucking hens and grunting piglets, and splendid beech woods all around the village. Our Louise was there too, and the two lively little girls, aged two.

Raimund did not stay there long. Albrecht brought him to America. Raimund was lucky. Because he spoke perfect Spanish, he was offered a professorship in physics, first in Caracas, Venezuela, and then at the Universidad de los Andes in Mérida. Mérida lies six thousand feet up in the tropics at the foot of the mighty, snow- and ice-covered Monte Bolívar. His family followed him around 1938. I arrived in New York in July 1938. I just missed seeing him there at the Fischers’.

In January 1973 I visited Raimund and Louise in Mérida. They lived on their own property outside the little town. It was a tropical Alpine idyll. Bougainvillea in bloom in every color, purple, yellow, pink, red, and white, surrounded the house and the studio and filled the garden. The lushness was beyond compare. There was an atmosphere of prosperity and contentment, even though Raimund was beginning to feel less than well.

I am writing this at the end of November 1988. We both still live peacefully in our own homes, despite age and illness and a troubled world around us. I am in California and Raimund is in Venezuela. We are the ones still left. We are the youngest of the six siblings.

## Gutrune

**Related biographies** · [Gutrune Götze](../../people/goetze/gutrune-goetze.md)

Born in Naunhof near Leipzig, June 8, 1903. Married Theodor Falcke (Theodore Falckon) on November 11, 1942. He was born in Dresden on January 1, 1896, and died in Berkeley, California, on February 12, 1971.

I was seventeen when my father died. Since my mother received only a small widow’s pension, she broke up the household and sold most of the furniture. Then she followed my brother Gottfried to the Bergfried settlement. There she became the cook for the whole settlement. She worked with a daughter of the well-known aviator Lilienthal.

I had just finished a one-year course as a gardener. I wanted to settle with Gottfried later. As it happened, I immediately got a job at a nursery next to the horticultural school in Zwingenberg an der Bergstrasse. The owner was Lene Knauer, who became my best friend for many years. I owe her a great deal.

Unfortunately there was an outbreak of whooping cough in Zwingenberg. Since I had not had it as a child, I fell very ill. I worked during the day and coughed at night until I collapsed. Lene, who was a very kind person, took me to a hospital in Darmstadt, where her sister had died of rheumatic fever a few years earlier. The hospital admitted me as a patient under the local health insurance fund. Because I was a doctor’s orphan, they gave me the very best care for six weeks. Lene took me back in. Air, sunshine, and light work soon made me strong enough to become her best assistant. I worked with a few other young people. It was a happy life.

Then, through my mother, I got a job as a gardening assistant at the nursery of the very wealthy Mr. Miller, who owned the large sawmill in Oderberg, in the Mark Brandenburg, Prussia. Mother was his housekeeper because Mrs. Miller had left him. Mr. Miller proposed to my mother. She managed to turn him down and reconcile him with his wife. Mother then left. Out of gratitude they kept me on until spring, when I went back to Lene Knauer.

Meanwhile Mother nursed our benefactor Laura Krause, who died in the spring. Uncle Krause also wanted to marry Mother, but she returned to Heppenheim to stay with Irmgard. Irmgard’s parents, Hermann and Gertrud, both went to Berlin. Hermann hoped to find a position there. I was back in Zwingenberg and often visited Mother in nearby Heppenheim. Raimund often came from Frankfurt and Gertraute from Heidelberg.

At that time I was engaged to Hans Plass. After teacher training, he became a village schoolteacher in the Odenwald. He was the fourth son of a north German pastor who had retired early because of consumption, and of his wife. They were very kind, gentle, friendly people, very musical and cultured. When the children were small, they had lived mostly in Italian-speaking Switzerland. Even as a schoolboy Hans played the organ in church when his father was too ill to do so. (His father had a small post as organist in the Protestant church in Heppenheim.)

I also spent a year in Stockstadt am Alt-Rhein with a pastor’s family who had nine children. The parsonage was said to be haunted. No housekeeper wanted to stay for that reason. The piano in the music room would start playing, and a big white dog would lie on the carpet beside it.

My school friend Hilde asked if I would come to stay with them. Her father had taken the pastor’s post there. He had been court and village pastor to the Prince of Schoenberg, a beautiful castle and little village near Bensheim an der Bergstrasse, Hesse. Meanwhile Germany had become a republic. The former prince could no longer pay the pastor’s salary and the post was abolished.

After the First World War, Stockstadt belonged to the territory occupied by French and Moroccan troops. It was right on the border. You had to go through a mile of “no man’s land” before reaching unoccupied German territory. The pastors’ salaries were smuggled across the border. Hilde and I walked from village to village to distribute the money to the local pastors. It must have been arranged differently later.

The Moroccans were mostly indifferent. They said, “Moi Marocain,” which meant, “We are not French. We are a conquered people too.”

Of course Hilde and I were very curious about the haunting, but we could never find anything of the kind. Hilde once had to sleep in the lonely attic room. I asked, “Did you notice anything?” The answer was, “No, only rats.” But the pastor’s wife told us, somewhat amused, “Father came home deathly pale from church after the late evening service. He had seen an old woman in a headscarf sitting in a high carved armchair near the pulpit that was otherwise always empty. He did not know her at all. After the service he asked the caretaker who she had been. The caretaker answered, ‘Nobody sat there!’”

I often ran secretly through no man’s land to visit my mother and my future in-laws in Heppenheim, and Hans, who was also home on Sundays. But the engagement broke off. I was very unhappy and wanted to go as far away as I possibly could. So I went to Bartenstein in East Prussia as a housekeeper for district court councillor Burath and his family. Lene Knauer had recommended me to them indirectly through Johannes Müller of Elmau.

It was a completely different world, very formal and lonely. There was green farmland with larks singing high in the blue sky, and old castles of the knights of the former Order. The Buraths also showed me Koenigsberg, the old northern city, and in summer the Baltic Sea. That was my first sight of the Atlantic Ocean.

From Bartenstein I went to Berlin to Gertraute, who was also very unhappy because of a love affair. Hermann had meanwhile found a good job in Berlin and Irmgard was back with her parents. Mother later came to Gertraute and me. We lived in Spandau with my sister’s little son. Aunt Krause had died, and instead of marrying Uncle Krause, Mother came to us to raise Franzl. He was born on September 10, 1927.

Then came Adolf Hitler. Franzl died of diphtheria in 1934. Mother died in April 1935. Gertraute went to America. I stayed in Berlin another five years [as written]. I had become a practitioner of therapeutic exercise because I could not earn a living as a gardener in Berlin. In 1938 I went to America too.

In the United States I could not get a license in physical therapy. So I worked as a masseuse in women’s reducing salons and soon built up a good private clientele. In 1942 I married Theo, whom I had met through Walter and Gertraute. Theo came from a very cultured and prosperous family in Dresden. Impoverished by German inflation after the First World War, he had already gone to America in 1924. He lived in New York.

In 1944, during the Second World War, we moved to Los Angeles. Nine years later we went to New Zealand. Theo was over fifty and could no longer find a job in America. In New Zealand I could not work as a physical therapist without first passing the local state examination. I was admitted to the school of physical therapy in Dunedin. After a year and a half of classes at the university and volunteer work in the university hospital, I was allowed to take the examination. The course normally takes five years. I passed, and after applying I became a member of the American Physical Therapy Association, which belongs to the international association. Through an advertisement in the American monthly physical therapy journal, I got a hospital job on the Big Island of Hawaii.

We were already in Auckland, New Zealand, waiting for the ship to Honolulu, when a telegram came: “Regret immensely! Native Physical Therapist applied for position.” [A handwritten German note paraphrases this as a Hawaiian physical therapist applying for the position.]

We went to Honolulu anyway. The official rule there was that I first had to work under the supervision of a U.S. physical therapist for a year to qualify for the territorial examination. But nobody wanted to hire me. The physical therapists were mostly Japanese and, in my view, feared any competition. The physical therapy inspector for the Territory of Hawaii, however, was German and took me along to the monthly physical therapy meetings. The Army physical therapists were also there, along with their chief, Major Tinton, a woman [handwritten name, reading uncertain].

A polio epidemic broke out in the Far East, especially among American occupation troops in Japan and their families. Tripler Army Hospital in Honolulu was overflowing. I was taken on as a volunteer physical therapist and ate in the officers’ dining room. The Army physical therapists were all lieutenants, and we worked very hard.

At the end of the year I received a glowing reference. I was admitted to the territorial examination, passed, and became director of physical therapy at the modern St. Francis Hospital, in a wonderful location in Honolulu. It was very beautiful there, though hot and humid. Hawaii was a territory and at the time, as I understood it, came under the District of Columbia. In other words it was federal. So I was entitled to work in a federal hospital in any state of the U.S. without having to take another examination. There are Veterans Administration hospitals in every state.

[Marginal note: “By the end we had almost no money left. But years before, I had bought Bethlehem Steel shares at $10 a share. They went up to $100 a share. We sold them and lived on the proceeds until I got the job at St. Francis Hospital. Theo had also earned a little as a night watchman in the girls’ dormitory at the University of Honolulu.”]

During my two weeks of vacation I set out. I flew to California and applied to every federal hospital there. A year later I got a telegram telling me to report immediately to the veterans’ hospital in Oakland, Northern California. Of course I could not come that quickly, but two months later we were on our way to Oakland. That was how I became a federal employee with all the financial benefits that came with it at the time. I stayed for twelve years and became eligible for a small pension. I retired at sixty-seven, in 1970, just in time to care for my husband, who died in 1971. I still live in the same house in Berkeley that we bought together in 1960.

## Closing remarks and explanations

From Dieter Goetze, Albrecht’s son, a retired engineer in Santa Barbara, California, I learned the following. Of course I do not know whether it has anything to do with Uncle Bruno. Dieter said his father had told him “that around 1810 a textile center, a branch of the Goetze textile business in Glauchau, Saxony, had been founded in New York.”

If it was as early as 1810, the founder must have been Carl Friedrich Goetze, master weaver and merchant, my great-grandfather Goetze. If it was somewhat later, it must have been the factory owner Emil Hermann, my grandfather.

It is a fact that we received packages of rice and canned meat from America in the First World War. We children were not allowed to know who had sent them and were strictly forbidden to talk about them.

It is a fact that Albrecht knew the address of a Goetze family in “York” [possibly New York]. They were stockbrokers. When Albrecht’s life was in danger in 1933, he asked them for an affidavit for the United States. They refused him. The stockbrokers must have been elderly by then.

To my knowledge Albrecht visited the Heinigke family in Altenburg, Saxony, around the same time. I suspect this must also have been on account of their connections in America. [See Heinigke, page 15.] The Heinigke family had been protectors and friends of the Waldensians and Huguenots for centuries, and through their help members of these groups became Lutheran pastors in Saxony. Christiana Friederike von Roba was married to a Dr. Heinigke in Altenburg around 1770.

Huguenots emigrated to North America as well as to northeastern Germany. They had established a stronghold in New Rochelle, a town not very far from New York. Albrecht probably hoped to get an affidavit through them. But since he belonged to no religious organization, this was probably refused too. Fortunately, Albrecht then received a non-quota visa through Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut. He was offered the chair of Assyriology there.

See page 13. As Mother told me many years later, Augusta Arndt’s second daughter went to America and became a faith healer there. After her mother died, the Heinigke family probably sent her to New Rochelle, the Huguenot stronghold. (Christian Science was also founded in Boston around 1870.) Augusta died in 1874.

See page 17. It is reasonable to assume that Johann Arendt, of unknown origin, who immigrated to Elbing around 1740 with Maria Susanna von Neaus, was also French. His original name was probably Arénd or something similar.

See page 16. The Waldensians were a Protestant sect that had existed in southern France since the twelfth century. Peter Waldo, or Waldus, was the founder. He was a merchant in Lyon, born around 1170. The Waldensians seem later to have aligned themselves more closely with the Calvinists. Jean Calvin was also born in France. He lived much later, from 1509 to 1564. Luther lived at about the same time, 1483–1546.

The Huguenots were also a Reformed Christian sect, but much later, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. They played a major role in France for a while. Noble and wealthy people were Huguenots. Under Richelieu, however, France persecuted and expelled them around 1640. It was only around 1740 that Frederick the Great, king of Prussia, granted them a refuge in East Prussia.

See page 25. In Stuttgart a younger brother of our grandfather Emil Roemmler had a similar business, built up on Emil’s model. It mainly produced floral postcards. Emil’s brother died young. Mother and I, when I was a schoolgirl, once visited his widow, Aunt Elise, in the summer holidays at her summer house in the Black Forest. It was beautifully situated. She lived in Stuttgart with her daughter Trude. Aunt Trude was also a widow. Her children were Egon and Irene, as far as I remember. Egon and Raimund studied together at the University of Tübingen and were friends. Albrecht seems to have studied in Tübingen at some point too. Both [Albrecht and Raimund, as specified in the margin] were often Aunt Trude’s guests, and both spoke enthusiastically about Aunt Trude and Irene.

## Family members in other countries

The family tree shows that the Goetze and Roemmler families have always been people on the move. Even in earlier times they went back and forth between Frankfurt am Main or Wimpfen am Neckar and Glauchau or Mitweida, Saxony. They intermarried with French people, Huguenots, and others. Although most family members today are German and live in Germany, the family tree shows that about thirty-three relatives we know of are in other countries.

## Reader’s guide

> The following notes summarize the chronicle. Page references point to the German manuscript in the bilingual archival edition. Approximate dates and family traditions are identified as such.

### The chronicle at a glance

Gutrune Falckon, née Goetze, began writing this account in 1988. She drew on what she remembered, what relatives had told her, and what she had read in the family tree. A handwritten note records that her nephew Dieter Goetze received the chronicle on March 8, 1989. (pp. 1, 30)

The early chapters follow the Goetzes from farming and linen weaving to textile manufacturing in Glauchau. On her mother’s side, Gutrune traces the Roemmler family from a town musician in Mitweida to Emil Roemmler’s photography and fine-art printing business in Dresden. Two Roemmler sisters married two Goetze brothers, joining the families twice. (pp. 1–2, 5, 9–12)

The center of the memoir is the household of Rudolf and Else Goetze. Gutrune recalls her father’s sanatorium near Leipzig, the family’s loss of its property after a water-tower venture, the Krauses’ help, and the family’s years in Heppenheim. Her portraits of her siblings follow them through war, changing careers, and moves to other countries. (pp. 6–9, 17–30)

In the final chapters, Gutrune describes her own path from gardening and therapeutic exercise in Germany to physical therapy in New Zealand, Hawaii, and California. Her closing list shows how far the family had dispersed by the late 1980s. (pp. 31–37)

### Family guide

- **Paternal grandparents.** Emil Hermann Goetze (1827–1902), textile manufacturer in Glauchau, married Friederike Brunner of Frankfurt in 1857. Their surviving children were Frieda, Bruno, Rudolf, and Bernhard. (pp. 1–5)

- **Maternal grandparents.** Karl Emil Roemmler (1842–1941), court photographer and printer in Dresden, married Helene Arndt in 1862. Helene was the daughter of Gottfried Ludwig Arndt and Auguste Heinigke. (pp. 9–13)

- **Gutrune’s parents.** Dr. Rudolf Emil Goetze (1863–1920) married Else Roemmler (1868–1935), also called Elsa in the chronicle, in Dresden in 1892. Rudolf’s brother Bernhard married Else’s sister Johanna, called Hanne or Hannchen. (pp. 5–6, 9, 11, 17, 23, 33)

- **Hermann.** Trained in law, served as a Turkish interpreter during the First World War, and built an auditing business in Berlin. He married Gertrud van Hoboken-Schmidt. Their daughter was Irmgard. (pp. 19–22)

- **Gertraute.** Worked in business correspondence and at the Altes Museum. Her son Franzl died at seven. She emigrated to the United States and married Walter Fischer. (pp. 22–24)

- **Albrecht.** An Assyriologist who left Nazi Germany and joined Yale University. The closing family list names his children Dieter Goetze, Marianne Pfeiffer, and Alle Lee. (pp. 24–26, 35–37)

- **Gottfried.** A biologist and agricultural specialist who directed a beekeeping school and later taught in Bonn. He married Margret Ulbrich. Their daughter Lore married Manfred Raether and had two sons. Lore’s sister Gudrun is also named in the closing family list through her daughter Heidi. (pp. 27–28, 37)

- **Raimund.** A physicist whose career took him from Frankfurt and Barcelona to Venezuela. He married Louise Lagrange. Their daughters Yvonne and Rolanda were twins. The closing list also names a daughter, Susanna. (pp. 29–30, 37)

- **Gutrune and Gunter.** Gutrune emigrated to the United States in 1938 and married Theodor Falcke, also named Theodore Falckon, in 1942. Her twin brother, Gunter, died a few days after birth. (pp. 17, 24, 31, 33–35)

### Selected timeline

- **1550 and 1589.** The chronicle places Moritz Goetze in Kuhschnappel near Eger in 1550 and records Adam Brunner’s marriage to Dorothea Schweitzer in Frankfurt in 1589. (p. 1)

- **1857, 1862, and 1892.** Emil Hermann Goetze marries Friederike Brunner. Karl Emil Roemmler marries Helene Arndt. A generation later, Rudolf Goetze marries Else Roemmler. (pp. 2, 6, 10)

- **1903–1920.** Gutrune is born in Naunhof in 1903. The family settles in Heppenheim by 1908. Rudolf dies in Alzey in 1920. (pp. 2–3, 6–9, 31)

- **1927–1938.** Gertraute’s son Franzl is born in 1927 and dies in 1934. Else dies in 1935. Albrecht has already left Germany by then. Gutrune arrives in New York in July 1938. (pp. 23–25, 30, 33, 35–36)

- **1942–1971.** Gutrune marries Theo in 1942. They move to Los Angeles in 1944, then to New Zealand and Hawaii before returning to California. She retires in 1970. Theo dies in 1971. (pp. 31, 34–35)

- **1973–1989.** Gutrune visits Raimund and Louise in Mérida in January 1973. She writes the chronicle in 1988. Dieter receives it in March 1989, according to the handwritten note. (pp. 1, 26, 30)

### Leads for family research

- **A possible New York textile branch.** Gutrune passes on Dieter’s account that Albrecht had heard of a New York branch of the Glauchau textile business, perhaps around 1810. She proposes two possible founders depending on the date. The branch’s existence, date, and connection to Bruno remain open questions. (pp. 5, 35)

- **Portraits, a Bible, and memoirs.** Gutrune points to Alle Lee’s archive as a possible home for portraits and Friederike Brunner’s account of her youth. She says Friederike’s Bible passed from Gertraute to Dieter and recalls reading Emil Roemmler’s memoir in Raimund’s Mérida studio. (pp. 2–3, 10, 14)

- **Maria Susanna’s surname and origins.** The manuscript gives De Noël or von Neaus and contains a marginal note questioning the first letter. Gutrune’s proposed French Protestant connection is a family hypothesis, and the marriage line bears a correction. (pp. 15–17, 36)

- **Dates that need a second source.** Gottfried Ludwig Arndt and Auguste Heinigke’s marriage year is typed as 1941, while their stated ages suggest 1841. Raimund and Louise’s marriage date is typed as 1943, but the subsequent narrative puts their marriage and their twins’ birth before the family’s departure from Spain during the Spanish Civil War. (pp. 13, 29–30)
