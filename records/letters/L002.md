Dr. Hermann Götze, Doctor of Law and Public Accountant
Mittelstraße 18, Berlin NW 7

## English translation of the enclosure

### PDF page 4

```text
Plant-breeding institutes, both private and state-run, are to agriculture what laboratories are
to the chemical industry. Plant breeding is as old as the deliberate cultivation of plants itself.
But it only came into its own once scientists began applying rigorous research methods. Here,
science and practice work hand in hand, and the results are remarkable. Over the past half
century, yields from German fields have increased by 60 to 80 percent. The growing use of
artificial fertilizers accounts for the largest share, about half of the increase. Plant breeding
accounts for another third, while the remainder comes from better soil cultivation, the use of
machinery, greenhouse construction, and similar improvements.
The main methods of plant breeding are selection and crossing. Selection means choosing
outstanding individual plants from among the many of their variety, provided they also pass
on their desirable traits. Higher-yielding oats, for example, were selected in this way. Crossing
two different species or varieties aims to combine the desirable traits of both parents in the
new plant and eliminate the undesirable ones. The sweet lupin and the sweet tomato are
textbook examples of what these methods can achieve.
The sweet lupin was developed by selecting plants from the bitter lupin. Despite its high
starch content, the bitter lupin could not be used as livestock feed because it contained too
many bitter compounds. Breeders in Müncheberg are still working to eliminate a few
undesirable traits in the new sweet lupin, such as pods that burst open too early. Even so, the
development of this new variety, which provides a particularly protein-rich feed, is essentially
complete.
The area planted with sweet lupins is increasing year by year. In 1935, 12,200 hectares were
planted in the Reich, and by 1936 the figure had already reached 25,000 hectares. For now,
the aim is to produce enough seed for the sweet lupin to replace the bitter lupin everywhere.
Within a few years, however, it could be fed to livestock, providing a homegrown source of
green fodder and helping avoid the costly import of 1.2 million metric tons of oilseed cake for
protein feed.
```

Plant breeders have already set themselves a new task. At present, clover pollination still
depends largely on visits from bumblebees, which we cannot control. The honeybee’s tongue
is generally too short to reach into the clover blossom. The challenge, then, is to breed either a
clover with flowers shallow enough for the honeybee’s tongue or a honeybee whose tongue is
long enough to reach into the clover flower. The Reich Minister of Food recently made
substantial funds available for such breeding experiments. If we could bring clover
and honeybees together, we would increase honey yields, secure a reliable clover seed harvest,
and avoid importing clover seed. Scientists estimate the value of that achievement at half a
billion marks.
The aim is also to improve existing crops by increasing their yields and making them more
resistant to disease and pests. Potatoes resistant to potato wart disease have already been
bred, and work is underway to develop frost-resistant potatoes. Starting in 1940, the Reich
Food Estate’s official variety list will no longer allow the sale of seed potatoes from varieties
susceptible to potato wart disease, so the disease will soon disappear from Germany. The
change cannot be made any faster because the widely grown but highly susceptible potato
variety “Industrie” cannot be replaced all at once.
